We will prove that there is no such $n$.
Assume the contrary. Obviously, the members of $\{n, n+1, n+2, \dots, n+8\}$ can have prime factors $p \le 7$ only. Otherwise exactly one member contains this factor and hence only one product will have this factor.
Among the 9 numbers there are exactly 5 odd if $n$ is odd or there are exactly 4 odd if $n$ is even. In any case there are 4 odd numbers all greater or equal 2, i.e., these 4 numbers are all products of the primes 3, 5 and 7 only. Two odd integers, which are multiples of 5 or 7, differ by at least $2 \times 5$ or $2 \times 7$, respectively. Therefore, exactly one of the 4 odd numbers is divisible by 5 or 7, respectively. Consequently, there are two of the 4 odd numbers which have prime factor 3 only, i.e., are powers of 3 (like 3 and 9). Among the 9 numbers there is exactly one divisible by 9. Therefore the other number divisible by 3 must be 3 itself. Eventually, 3 is a member of the set, i.e. the set is $\{1, 2, \dots, 9\}$, $\{2, 3, \dots, 10\}$ or $\{3, 4, \dots, 11\}$. In every case there is exactly one member divisible by 7, which is impossible.
