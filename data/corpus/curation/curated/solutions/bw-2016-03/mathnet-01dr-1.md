**Answer:** Solutions exist for $n = 1, 2, 3$.

For $n = 6$, we consider the equation $a^6 + b^6 = c^6 + 6$ modulo 13. We always have $x^6 \equiv 0, 1$ or $-1$ (mod 13) (by Fermat's little theorem or a direct computation). However, then it is evident that $a^6 + b^6 - c^6$ cannot be 6 (mod 13).

For $n = 5$, we consider the equation $a^5 + b^5 = c^5 + 5$ modulo 11. We always have $x^5 \equiv 0, 1$ or $-1$ (mod 11) (by Fermat's little theorem or a direct computation). This is a contradiction just as in the previous case.

For $n = 4$, we consider the equation $a^4 + b^4 = c^4 + 4$ modulo 8. Since always $x^4 \equiv 0, 1$ mod 8, we clearly have a contradiction as in the previous cases.

For $n = 1, 2, 3$, there are solutions
$$
1^1 + 0^1 = 0^1 + 1, \quad 1^2 + 1^2 = 0^2 + 2, \quad 1^3 + 1^3 = (-1)^3 + 3.
$$
